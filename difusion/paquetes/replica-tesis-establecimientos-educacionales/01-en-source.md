# Transcreated English Source — Part I: Replicating My 2014 Thesis on School Entry and Exit in Chile (What Survived and What the Evidence Corrected)

> Canonical URL: `https://3cucharadas.cl/en/datos/educacion/politica-publica/replica-tesis-establecimientos-educacionales/`

In April 2014, I defended my master's thesis on the entry and exit of schools in Chile between 1992 and 2012. Twelve years later, before extending the panel by thirteen additional years through 2025, I asked a more basic question: **which of my 2014 results could I reproduce exactly, which were wrong back then, and why?**

Auditing yourself is not an independent replication, and nothing in this first instalment is causal. In Chile's administrative school directory (`MINEDUC`), each school has an official identifier (`RBD`, *Rol Base de Datos*), school-level aggregate socioeconomic vulnerability is tracked by `IVE` (*Índice de Vulnerabilidad Escolar*), and learning is measured by the national standardised assessment (`SIMCE`). Across **34** editorial judgements and **50** technical hypotheses (spanning **26** display objects: **12** convergences, **10** explained divergences, and **4** without a numerical test), **15** judgements held up, **10** errors came from the 2014 thesis document, and **0** known errors remain in the 2026 reference output. Of the remaining **9** claims, **1** was wrong on both sides, **1** was a misleading match, and **7** lacked sufficient provenance to name a winner (**3** matched once a common comparison basis was established, **2** kept differences, and **4** lacked a common basis).

The audit distils one methodological boundary for empirical public policy: **rerunnable does not mean rebuildable; rebuildable does not mean matching; matching does not mean correct; correct does not mean causal**.

---

## 1. What Survived: Sectoral Reallocation and Distributive Sorting (1992–2012)

Two structural conclusions held up when rebuilt from raw directories and compared against the original 2014 producer files (Stata logs, spreadsheets, and charts):
- **Sectoral reallocation rather than net expansion:** Between 1992 and 2012, my 2026 reconstruction counts **2,168** school entries and **2,069** exits (compared with **2,160** and **2,065** in my 2014 workbook). Municipal public schools recorded **346** entries and **1,135** exits; subsidised private voucher schools recorded **1,468** entries and **597** exits; fee-paying private schools recorded **354** entries and **337** exits. In the 2012 cross-section, the aggregate share distance from the prior spreadsheet run is **3.05** percentage points, whereas the published text differed from the producing spreadsheet by **13.41** percentage points across the six shares (**4.50** points in municipal, **2.42** in free subsidised-private, and **4.27** in high-fee schools).
- **Distributive vulnerability in Greater Santiago:** Schools that later exited the register served more vulnerable enrolments (`IVE`): **87.4%** versus **78.3%** in the municipal sector, and **72.2%** versus **62.8%** in the subsidised-private sector (**42** rebuilt cells match within rounding, alongside **10** of **11** published fixed effects, **12** coefficients with the constant, a **−4.3%** `SIMCE` gap among closing subsidised schools, and sector mobility rates of **6.3**, **4.5**, and **1.6**).

## 2. Where My 2014 Self Was Wrong: Transcription, Scale Errors, and Mundlak–Chamberlain Decomposition

Comparing three separate layers—the published 2014 text, the surviving 2014 producer outputs, and present-day reruns in Stata and Python—isolated four instructive failures:
1. **Transcription and untransformed parameters in hierarchical models:** In Table 6, lagged mathematics `SIMCE` was printed as **0.003**, whereas the producing Stata log and new Stata/Python runs yield **0.000533**, which rounds to **0.001**. Two rows labelled as variances (**3.82** and a crossed log standard deviation) were actually untransformed log standard deviations printed by Stata: the true between-municipality random-intercept variance is **265.6** and the residual variance is **2,079.7** (while another cell turned **0.4142** into **1.4142**).
2. **Mundlak (1978) and Chamberlain (1982) Correlated Random Effects (`CRE`) vs. Collapsed Random Slope (`N = 14,704` observations across `2,640` Greater Santiago schools):** In 2014, the hierarchical model for log enrolment had a random-slope variance that collapsed onto the numerical boundary (`2.1 × 10⁻¹⁵`) and included three 2006 cross-sectional covariates with no within-school time variation, blending between-school sorting and within-school change into a single coefficient. Re-estimating by pooled OLS with the **Mundlak (1978) and Chamberlain (1982)** decomposition (separating each school's historical mean from its annual deviations, with `RBD`-clustered standard errors) splits the relationship in two: the positive association with lagged mathematics `SIMCE` comes entirely from permanent differences **between** schools (`0.012131`, `t = 16.27`), whereas **within** the same school over time, annual `SIMCE` changes are indistinguishable from zero and flip sign (`−0.000048`, `t = −0.53`; the dynamic specification yields `0.000543`, `t = 7.26` between and `0.000174`, `t = 2.44` within, subject to Nickell bias in short panels). Schools with higher historical `SIMCE` are larger, but a school that raises its `SIMCE` one year does not detectably gain enrolment the next.
3. **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (`HHI`) scale error:** Canonical `HHI` runs from **0** to **10,000** (with high-concentration thresholds of **2,500** under the 2010 US Merger Guidelines and **1,800** in 2023). My 2014 spreadsheet stored `HHI` times **100**, and the text read it as times one thousand—reporting Santiago around **1,534** instead of canonical **152**. A spreadsheet filter `> 2.5` (**250** canonical points) selected **330** of **346** municipalities in 2006 and **326** in 2016 (**95%**); across the **337** rebuilt panel municipalities, the published scale would classify **318** as highly concentrated versus **66** on the canonical scale (negative slope `p = 0.084`). Similarly, a claim that cities under **15,000** inhabitants concentrated **80%** of mobility reflected a capital/non-capital split (**78.3%**), whereas the literal population cutoff gave **11%**.
4. **The “shadow of death” in pupil–teacher ratios (`59,808` observations = `36,493` municipal + `23,315` subsidised-private, 2004–2011):** Across the seven years before exit, the pupil–teacher ratio of exiting schools falls from **17.95** to **12.22** (versus **20.31** among staying schools), operating with **5.90** fewer pupils per teacher in the municipal sector and **6.23** fewer in the subsidised-private sector, and contracting faster by **0.849** vs **0.632** (municipal, **0.216** additional annual fall) and **0.565** vs **0.417** (subsidised-private, **0.148** additional annual fall).

## 3. Out-of-Sample Temporal Validation and the Data-Inferred Closure Rule (`RCID v1`)

Before extending the series to 2025, I subjected the thesis's discrete-time hazard alert (`cloglog` on 2007–2011, Table 10: **30,484** school-year observations across **7,460** `RBD`s and **237** closures) to an out-of-sample temporal split—training on 2007–2008 (**12,495** rows, **86** closures) and testing on 2009–2011 (**17,989** rows, **151** closures):
- **Rank discrimination is strong (`AUC = 0.8517`, Hanley–McNeil interval `0.81–0.89`):** The top **10%** risk decile captures **43.7%** of actual closures (**66** of **151**), and the top **20%** quintile captures **69.5%**.
- **Probability calibration is weak:** Mean predicted risk is **0.60%** versus **0.84%** observed, and the Brier score (**0.008196**) improves by only **1.5%** over a naive base-rate forecast (**0.008324**). The model ranks monitoring priorities well, but cannot be used as an automatic closure rule.

Finally, linking individual student rosters from 2004 onward replaces raw directory exits (**2,069** in 1992–2012) with the three-tier **Data-Inferred School Closure Rule (`RCID v1`, 2005–2025)**: **D0** identifies **1,981** permanent closures with prior enrolment; **D1** removes annexes and directory continuities, leaving **1,807** strict closures; and **D2** removes block absorptions (`≥ 70%` of reassigned students moving to a single stable receiving school with destination `HHI ≥ 5,000`), leaving **1,678** conservative inferred closures (**1,382** in 2005–2015 and **296** in 2016–2025) and **1,648** clean closures with a fully observed `t+1` year (**1,382** and **266**, setting aside **30** right-censored 2025 episodes).
