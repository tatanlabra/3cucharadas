# What Happens to Students When a School Closes? Twenty Years of Administrative Trajectories, Institutional Regimes, and Missing-Data Bounds in Chile

*Originally published on [3 Cucharadas](https://3cucharadas.cl/en/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/) (in [Spanish](https://3cucharadas.cl/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/) and [English](https://3cucharadas.cl/en/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/)) as the third instalment of the “Extending My Thesis in Three Spoonfuls” series.*

When a school closes, policy discussions often stop at the map: if another school with open seats sits a mile away, the transition is assumed to be covered. Yet buildings on a map do not tell us whether displaced students stayed enrolled the following year, progressed to the expected grade, or absorbed a hidden disruption. Above all, a map cannot separate what happened *because* of the closure from trajectories that were already fraying before the school shut its doors.

In this third instalment, we move from school-level maps to individual student trajectories across twenty years of nationwide administrative records from Chile’s Ministry of Education (`MINEDUC`), linking **79,709,054 enrolment records** and **73,873,835 annual academic performance records** between 2004 and 2025.

The headline finding holds two empirical realities together:
- **Across the full twenty-year macro window (`2005–2024`):** Following **78,972 student trajectories** in **1,141 regular schools** that closed between 2005 and 2024 against **734,472 controls** from schools that stayed open, the point estimate for **progression failure or grade lag**—having no enrolment record or being enrolled below the expected grade one year later—is positive in **all 19 estimable annual cohorts** (`2006–2024`, ranging from **+0.94** to **+28.22 percentage points**, median **+7.18**). Aggregating the full series weighted by exposed students (**Callaway and Sant'Anna 2021**) yields a mean gap of **+9.11 percentage points** (95% CI: **+5.34 to +12.88**; assumption-free **Manski 2003** bounds: **+0.43 to +16.21 points**, which remain strictly above zero), alongside **+7.55 points** (**+5.52 to +9.57**) under equal annual cohort weights, **+6.29 points** (**+4.49 to +8.09**; prediction interval **+0.09 to +12.49**, *I*² = **76.5%**) under a random-effects model, and **+1.38 points** (**+0.92 to +1.85**) in two-year persistent non-enrolment.
- **Concentration and monotonic attenuation across five institutional regimes:** Nearly nine in ten supported closures (**1,023 of 1,141**, **89.7%**) and **86.7%** of exposed students (**68,496 of 78,972**) occurred during the first two regulatory eras: **2005–2010** (`LOCE` and early `SEP` rollout; **521** closures, **36,984** exposed, progression gap **+12.15 pp** **[+3.78, +20.52]** with Manski bounds **[+3.49, +18.83]** excluding zero) and **2011–2015** (`LGE` and `SAC` quality rollout; **502** closures, **31,512** exposed, **+7.82 pp** **[+6.20, +9.41]**, and a series peak of **+1.85 pp** in two-year non-enrolment). Thereafter, closure volume drops and the progression gap attenuates to **+5.29 pp** in **2016–2019** (**63** closures, **6,190** exposed; 95% CI **2.08 to 8.51**, Manski bounds **−1.15 to 10.98 pp**), **+2.70 pp** in **2020–2022** (**33** closures), and **+2.59 pp** in **2023–2024** (**22** closures).
- **Why the nested `2016–2019` pre-registered zoom (`5.5%` of closures, `7.8%` of exposed students) is partial, inconclusive evidence on its own—and what its three stress tests reveal:** In the `2016–2019` subwindow, the **+5.29 pp** progression estimate has overall Manski missing-data bounds of **−1.15 to 10.98 pp** that cross zero. However, within closures with at least **90%** valid baseline records at *G*−1 (**1,822** exposed students, **38%** of exposed support), missingness equalises (**2.31%** vs. **2.15%**), the gap is **+4.26 pp**, and the Manski bounds stay strictly above zero (**+0.09 to +7.84 pp**, threshold **λ* = 1.0114**). Pre-closure placebos (**+0.16**, **+0.30**, **+0.40 pp**) show that post-closure bias would have to reach **2.07 pp** (**2.54** times the **0.81 pp** placebo upper bound) to nullify the confidence interval, and three-quarters of the gap persists among continuous stayers (**+3.87 pp** across **3,556** stayers vs. **+9.58 pp** across **1,033** late entrants). Still, because **47.04%** of exposed students had already switched schools between *G*−3 and *G*−1 (vs. **30.52%** of controls), observational difference-in-differences captures a mix of closure disruption and pre-existing institutional fragility.

## 1. Separating Paper Exits from Real Dispersions: The Data-Inferred School Closure Rule (`RCID v1`)

In massive administrative panels covering thousands of schools over two decades, a school’s administrative identifier (`RBD`, *Rol Base de Datos*) can disappear from the directory because of an annex consolidation, a site relocation, or a *de facto* merger where the entire student body moves together to a single receiving school.

To ensure complete consistency across all three instalments (`2005–2025`), we formalise a falsifiable three-tier hierarchy—the **Data-Inferred School Closure Rule (`RCID v1`, D0 ⊃ D1 ⊃ D2)**:

| Rule stratum | Historical window (2005–2015) | Recent window (2016–2025) | Full longitudinal series (2005–2025) | Operational definition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **D0:** Raw administrative exit | 1,635 | 346 | 1,981 | Positive enrolment in *t*−1, zero in *t*, and no subsequent teaching activity. |
| **D1:** Strict closure of educational provision | 1,477 | 330 | 1,807 | Purges 158 historical and 16 recent annexes, secondary sites, and registered `RBD` successors. |
| **D2 = `RCID v1` (conservative)** | 1,382 | 296 | 1,678 | Sets aside 95 historical and 34 recent concentrated transfers: ≥ 10 baseline and ≥ 10 assigned students, ≥ 70% to a single receiver, destination HHI ≥ 0.50, and a stable main destination in *t* and *t*+1. |
| **D2 = `RCID v1` (clean, observed *t*+1)** | 1,382 | 266 | 1,648 | Further requires observing the full *t*+1 year (excluding the 30 right-censored 2025 closures). |

Of the **1,648** clean `D2` closures, **1,177** enrolled students in regular primary or secondary grades and **1,141** found exact baseline controls (**78,972** exposed student-cohort observations vs. **734,472** controls). In the recent window (`2016–2025`), **346** raw exits pared down to **330** strict `D1` closures (**266** without contradictory signal, **15** continuity candidates, **35** unresolved alerts, **30** right-censored in 2025, with **45** cross-cutting concentrated-flow alerts and **0** external confirmations), and **145** of the **266** clean `D2` closures served exclusively early-childhood (**69**), special (**57**), adult (**13**), or terminal secondary (**1**, plus **5** dominated) modalities where regular grade progression does not apply.

## 3. Nested Zoom (2016–2019): Effective Sample Size, Pre-Closure Asymmetries, and Three Stress Tests

Why zoom into **2016–2019** if it represents only **5.5%** of closures (**63** of **1,141**) and **7.8%** of exposed students (**6,190** of **78,972**)? Because it was pre-registered before estimating the 20-year series (status codes standardised in 2015, multi-year pre-trends inside a panel starting in 2004, and uncontaminated by the 2020–2022 pandemic). Across its **62 episodes** with observable primary follow-up:
- **Persistent non-enrolment for two years:** **+0.63 pp** (95% CI: **[−0.77, 2.03]**, **3,666** exposed vs. **49,944** controls; **+0.71 pp** **[−0.69, 2.12]** across **3,670** exposed and **59,613** controls when keeping receiving schools in the comparison pool).
- **Progression failure or grade lag (1 year later):** **+5.29 pp** (95% CI: **[2.08, 8.51]**, **4,815** exposed vs. **65,538** controls, rising from **2.2%** at *G*−2 to **15.8%** at *G*+1 vs. **10.6%** implied by controls—a **50%** relative increase or roughly **255** students if causal; **+5.09 pp** **[1.94, 8.23]** across **4,822** exposed and **79,058** controls with receiving schools included), but **inconclusive on its own** once overall **Manski missing-data bounds of [−1.15, 10.98] pp** are applied (**3.60%** missing among exposed vs. **2.15%** among controls).
- **Exploratory conditional outcomes (`58` episodes, `3,871` exposed, `62.5%` coverage):** attendance **−1.15 points** (**[−2.40, 0.10]**, **63,591** controls) and average mark **−0.06** (**[−0.14, 0.01]**, **63,596** controls). Weighted by students, the 62 closures carry the effective information of only **21.1 effective episodes** for non-enrolment, **21.9** for progression, and **17.7** for attendance and marks.

At *G*−1, individual demographics and poverty are tightly balanced: mean age is **11.70 years**, attendance **90.93%** vs. **91.68%**, average mark **5.62** vs. **5.67**, baseline over-age **33.86%** in both groups, priority `SEP` poverty **30.68%** vs. **32.32%** (**−1.64 pp**), and total `SEP` eligibility **54.51%** vs. **55.58%** (**−1.07 pp**). Yet institutional fragility and prior mobility diverge sharply: exposed students had lower valid *G*−1 record coverage (**76.8%** vs. **96.2%**, a **−19.4 pp** gap in the unweighted proportions, or **−19.24 pp** once controls are reweighted to the match, the figure used in the infographic), lower effective `SEP` receipt (**37.63%** vs. **48.58%**, **−10.95 pp**), fewer school `SEP` agreements (**45.17%** vs. **55.23%**, **−10.06 pp**), less Full School Day `JEC` (**53.97%** vs. **72.51%**, **−18.54 pp**), more inter-municipality commuting (**38.59%** vs. **29.99%**, **+8.60 pp**), and far higher prior school switching between *G*−3 and *G*−1 (**47.04%** vs. **30.52%**, **+16.52 pp**).

Three targeted stress tests clarify what drives the `2016–2019` zoom:
1. **High-coverage `G−1` closures (`≥ 90%` valid records, Supreme Decree No. `315` Art. `27` benchmark):** In closures where at least **90%** of students had valid *G*−1 records (**1,822** exposed students with an outcome, **38%** of exposed support), post-closure missingness equalises (**2.31%** vs. **2.15%**), the progression gap is **+4.26 pp**, and **Manski bounds span [+0.09, +7.84] pp**—strictly excluding zero (threshold **λ* = 1.0114**; dropping only closures below **50%** coverage leaves **[−0.90, +12.18] pp**).
2. **Pre-trend sensitivity (Rambachan and Roth 2023) and continuous stayers vs. late entrants:** Three pre-closure placebos yield **+0.16**, **+0.30**, and **+0.40 pp** (largest 95% upper bound **0.81 pp**); post-closure bias would need to reach **2.07 pp** (**2.54** times that upper bound) to push the confidence interval around **+5.29 pp** (bounds **[−1.15, 10.98] pp**) across zero. Separating students continuously enrolled in the closing school from *G*−2 to *G*−1 (**3,556** stayers, **77.5%** of those with observed grades and **70.6%** of all exposed) from late entrants (**1,033**) yields **+3.87 pp** (**[+1.04, +6.69]**) among stayers versus **+9.58 pp** (**[+5.06, +14.10]**) among late entrants.
3. **Prior quality of the receiving school (`ΔQ_i` at `G−1`):** Among **4,362** exposed students who re-enrolled in another school in the closure year (*G*), those transferring to a school with a higher historical promotion rate at *G*−1 (**2,099** students) show a progression gap of **+2.03 pp**, compared with **+5.38 pp** among those moving to a school with an equal or lower prior promotion rate (**2,263** students)—a descriptive difference of **−3.35 pp**.

## 4. Precautionary Safeguards for School Transitions

Neither staggered difference-in-differences nor candidate instruments (`SLEP`, `SAE`, or demographic shocks, which directly alter resources and sorting and thus fail the exclusion restriction) turn administrative records into a randomised trial, and higher-education outcomes await linked individual microdata from `SIES` and `DEMRE` (handled in Part IV). Yet an inconclusive bound in the recent subwindow and a positive twenty-year gap shift the burden of proof onto educational authorities: verify real seats, active `SEP` agreements, Full School Day (`JEC`) provision, and safe transport travel times before closing a school, preserve peer continuity during the transition, and track every displaced student by name for at least two years afterward.
