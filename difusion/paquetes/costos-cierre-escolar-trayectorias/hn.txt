What student trajectories show after a school closure in Chile (2004–2025)

URL:
https://3cucharadas.cl/en/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/?utm_source=hackernews&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias

PRIMER COMENTARIO DEL AUTOR:
Author here. This is Part III of a series revisiting and extending my master's thesis on school closures in Chile using twenty years of nationwide administrative microdata from the Ministry of Education (2004 to 2025, processed locally in DuckDB).

In Part II we looked at school directories and geography and found that the nearest school on a map is rarely where displaced students actually go. In Part III we lead with the full twenty-year macro window (2005 to 2024) across five institutional regimes, following individual student trajectories before and after closure using exact baseline matching (year, sex, region, provider type, rurality, level, and grade) and staggered difference-in-differences (Callaway & Sant'Anna 2021; Sun & Abraham 2021).

Four methodological findings stood out while building the verification gates for this piece:

1. Directory exits are not the same as real closures with student dispersion. Tracking student flows across twenty years shows many cases where most students move together into a single receiving school (de facto mergers or site continuations). Filtering those out via a Data-Inferred School Closure Rule (RCID v1) separates concentrated block absorptions from genuine student dispersion.

2. Across the full twenty-cohort series (2005–2024), all nineteen estimable annual progression cohorts are positive, and worst-case Manski (2003) missing-data bounds on the full-series Callaway & Sant'Anna student-weighted aggregate remain strictly above zero. Dividing the two decades into five institutional stages shows that nearly nine in ten closures occurred between 2005 and 2015, while the post-closure progression gap attenuated monotonically from 2005–2010 and 2011–2015 to 2016–2019 and the post-2020 cohorts.

3. In our nested pre-registered recent zoom (2016–2019), point estimates look statistically significant while being substantively inconclusive on their own once missing outcomes are bounded across the full subwindow. However, restricting that zoom to closures meeting the ninety-percent valid baseline record benchmark equalises missingness and keeps worst-case Manski bounds strictly above zero; three-quarters of the gap persists among continuous stayers who had not switched schools prior to closure; and students relocated into schools with higher prior promotion rates show a smaller progression gap than those absorbed by equal-or-lower-performing schools (a descriptive contrast, since families choose the destination and the difference has no confidence interval of its own yet).

4. Even so, extended baseline profiling shows that nearly half of exposed students had already switched schools in the two years before closure and attended schools with fewer institutional subsidies and less full-day schooling, so the pattern is an adjusted observational association rather than a natural experiment.

All figures have light/dark responsive SVGs and open CSV data tables linked directly below each chart. The analysis code lives in a private thesis repository for now; the aggregated figure data is open. Happy to answer questions about the DuckDB pipeline, the econometric bounds, or Chile's voucher and public school reforms.

NOTAS INTERNAS — NO PUBLICAR
Objeciones probables y respuesta preparada:
1. “Why didn't you match on prior school switching or school-level subsidies if you saw imbalance there?” → Conditioning on prior mobility or school-level supply attributes inside exact strata rapidly shrinks common support in rural municipalities and risks conditioning on pre-closure anticipation as families leave a deteriorating school before the formal closure year. Instead of hiding that trade-off inside a propensity score, we kept the exact demographic and grade strata transparent, exposed the institutional and mobility gaps, separated continuous stayers from late entrants, and paired the estimates with Rambachan & Roth (2023) pre-trend sensitivity.
2. “Aren't Manski worst-case bounds too conservative?” → Worst-case Manski bounds are intentionally agnostic: they show what the data alone can guarantee without untestable missing-at-random assumptions. Presenting the full 2005–2024 series (where worst-case bounds on the Callaway & Sant'Anna aggregate and on the 2005–2010 regime stay strictly positive), the overall 2016–2019 zoom (where bounds cross zero), and the high-record-coverage 2016–2019 subset (where bounds again stay positive) lets readers see exactly where missingness can flip the sign and where it cannot.
3. “Couldn't you use the rollout of public education boards (SLEP) or centralized admissions (SAE) as an instrument for closure?” → We audited both candidates and rejected them in the identification gate. Both SLEP and SAE directly change school funding, seat allocation, and student sorting across the local market—violating the exclusion restriction.
