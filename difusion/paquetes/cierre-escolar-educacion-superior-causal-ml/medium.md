# Losing a school is not the end of the educational story

Finding another classroom does not tell us whether a student will continue into higher education. In Chile, I linked school records with the Higher Education Information Service (SIES) to examine that longer trajectory.

The comparison follows two generations at the same grade in each secondary school: one enrolled five years before a loss-of-provision event and another enrolled one year before it. I then compare their changes with those of schools without a detected event in the inventory. These are different generations, not repeated measurements of the same students.

## Four rates tell more than one gap

For events in 2009–2015, observed higher-education enrolment was 45.87% for the earlier generation and 48.56% for the later generation in schools with an event. The corresponding control rates were 60.43% and 65.43%. The adjusted aggregate contrast between changes was −2.31 percentage points, with a 97.5% interval from −5.26 to +0.29.

For 2016–2024, the event-school rates were 55.93% and 55.01%, compared with 66.17% and 67.68% in controls. The aggregate contrast was −2.43 points, with a 97.5% interval from −5.67 to +1.27.

Both generations in event schools show a disadvantage in levels. Neither interval establishes a direction for the overall access contrast. Crossing zero is not evidence that the groups are equal, and an estimated difference is not automatically an effect of losing a school.

## The calendar is part of the question

Enrolment is measured during the two years after expected secondary-school completion. Expected completion is calculated from grade progression; it is not verified graduation. A missing linked SIES record in that window does not prove that a person never studied or will never enrol later.

The earlier generation is not a perfectly clean pre-event outcome: some follow-up windows reach the event year. Controls may receive displaced students. Choosing controls that experience a future event changes the comparison population and can change the sign. These are reasons to retain uncertainty, not to pick the estimate that tells the neatest story.

## Access, destination and continuity are different outcomes

I distinguish university only, professional institute or technical training centre only, both types, and no linked enrolment record. An aggregate shift between categories does not demonstrate that individuals substituted one pathway for another.

Persistence means enrolment in at least two of the three years beginning after expected school completion. Degree attainment uses six inclusive calendar years. Both retain the initial eligible population, including students without observed higher-education access; neither is a rate among entrants. Recent generations cannot supply the same mature follow-up as older ones.

The historical contrasts show descriptive differences in persistence and attainment. Modern estimates rely on fewer schools and allow a wide range of outcomes. Further checks do not turn these comparisons into causal estimates: doubly robust difference-in-differences did not meet estimability requirements under the specified design, and the flexible machine-learning route was not run.

## What the records can support

A policy discussion needs to distinguish finding a vacancy from following a student's later trajectory. These records describe inequalities and show how conclusions depend on population, calendar and comparator. They do not yet quantify how much of the trajectory changed because a school lost its provision.
