When a high school closes its doors in the middle of secondary education, a family's concern does not end with finding a seat for the following March. International research has extensively documented the short-run costs of closing schools on attendance, grade retention, and standardized test scores (Engberg et al. 2012[^engberg2012]; Brummet 2014[^brummet2014]; Grau, Hojman, and Mizala 2018[^grau2018]; Beuchert et al. 2018[^beuchert2018]; Bross, Harris, and Liu 2023[^bross2023]), as well as the decisive role of receiving-school quality (Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak, and Walters 2026[^abdulkadiroglu2026]). Yet evidence on what happens years later when students cross into higher education remains far scarcer and more contested (Larsen 2020[^larsen2020]; Kim 2024[^kim2024]). The deeper question in Chile is what unfolds two, three, or five years later when it is time to cross that postsecondary bridge: do those young people enroll in a university, a professional institute (IP), or a technical training center (CFT)? Do they persist into their second year or drop out along the way? And how much of the gap observed at the end of the road was genuinely caused by the school closure, versus how much was already written years earlier in the fragility of the school and the student's prior academic trajectory?
{: .text-justify}

In [part III of this series]({{ '/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/' | relative_url }}), I left that question explicitly open because the download portals of the Ministry of Education (MINEDUC) and the Department of Educational Assessment, Measurement and Registration (DEMRE) had suffered service interruptions and I did not yet have the verified individual linkage with the Higher Education Information Service (SIES). In this fourth installment I close that full cycle: I recovered and cryptographically verified all **20 annual SIES Higher Education Enrollment rosters (2007–2026)**, all **19 SIES Graduation rosters (2007–2025)**, and **6 centralized PAES/PDT admission cycles published by MINEDUC under an open CC-BY license (2021–2026)**, linking them to **79,709,054 student-year school enrollment records** and **73,873,835 academic performance records** between 2004 and 2025.
{: .text-justify}

The central finding resembles an onion that must be peeled layer by layer. If one directly compares secondary students (Grades 9 to 11, 1.º a 3.º Medio) whose high school closed between 2016 and 2019 against students in the same grade, region, rurality, and school governance whose school remained open, an alarming cliff appears: **their timely entry into higher education is 13 to 15 percentage points lower** (50.61% versus 65.24%, a raw difference of **−14.64 percentage points** within exact cells, or **−13.24 percentage points** in the first demographic-adjusted rung of my model, with a 95% confidence interval of **−19.00 to −7.48 pp**). It would be tempting to stop at that headline and conclude that closing a high school single-handedly destroys thirteen or fourteen points of postsecondary access. Yet that conclusion would commit a classic statistical fallacy: **confusing the hospital with the disease**—judging a hospital by patient mortality without noticing that the patients arriving at its doors were already much sicker beforehand.
{: .text-justify}

**Plain-language quick guide (ELI5) before reading the numbers.** So that no acronym or symbol gets in the way of the story, it helps to distinguish two simple clocks used throughout this post:
{: .text-justify}

- **The school-closure clock (with a minus sign: $$G$$, $$G-1$$, $$G-2$$, and $$G-5$$):** I denote by **$$G$$** the calendar year in which a school closes its doors; **$$G-1$$** is its last year operating with students; **$$G-2$$** is two years before closure; and **$$G-5$$** is an earlier cohort enrolled in that same school four years before the exposed cohort that, in the secondary grades, graduated before the school shut down. I call **expected higher-education entry year ($$t_0$$)** the calendar year immediately following on-time twelfth-grade graduation if the student progressed one grade per year without repeating.
- **The five student-vulnerability groups (without a minus sign: G1 to G5):** When I sort students into five equal-sized groups (20% quintiles) according to the individual harm predicted by the model, **G1** denotes the 20% most harmed, **G3** the middle quintile, and **G5** the 20% most resilient.
- **Institutional acronyms:** **SIES** is Chile's official administrative registry covering all higher education (universities, professional institutes **IP**, and technical training centers **CFT**); **PAES/PDT** is the national university admission exam; **NEM** is high-school grade-point average; and **pp** stands for percentage points (the arithmetic difference between two percentages). Methodological explanations are placed in highlighted callout boxes like this one so they never interrupt the main narrative, which unfolds in **three spoonfuls and a closing section**: (1) how I settled the open methodological debts from part III and built the postsecondary linkage; (2) how the causal decomposition ladder dismantles the 13-to-15-point illusion; and (3) how *Causal Machine Learning* uncovers who actually loses the postsecondary bridge.
</aside>

Once I unpack the prior history of both the student and the high school using modern econometrics (**repeated-cohort difference-in-differences**) and causal machine learning (**Double/Debiased Machine Learning** and the **Orthogonal R-Learner**; Chernozhukov et al. 2018[^chernozhukov2018]; Chernozhukov et al. 2024[^chernozhukov2024]; Wager 2024[^wager2024]), three facts emerge that completely reshape the public diagnosis:
{: .text-justify}

1. **Over four-fifths of the apparent gap already existed before the closure:** four years before the exposed cohort ($$G-5$$), earlier cohorts from those very same secondary schools were already entering higher education at a rate **12.00 percentage points lower** than their controls (51.78% versus 63.78%, representing 82.0% of the −14.64 pp raw cell gap). Accounting first for the student's own prior academic backpack in the two years before closure ($$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$: grade-point average, attendance, prior grade retention, and pre-closure mobility) cuts the adjusted gap by more than half, from −13.24 pp to **−5.63 pp** (interval −9.78 to −1.47 pp). Comparing against the $$G-5$$ cohort from the same high school lowers the gap to **−2.64 pp** in difference-in-differences (−7.54 to +2.25 pp); and accounting simultaneously for the student backpack and that historical baseline brings it to **−0.29 pp** in full *Double Machine Learning* (−2.64 to +2.06 pp), statistically indistinguishable from zero.
2. **The grade at which closure occurs governs recovery capacity:** when closure hits a student in **upper elementary school (Grades 7 to 8, 7.º a 8.º Básico)**, four or five years in a receiving school remain before high-school graduation. In that group, the net effect on higher-education entry is null or slightly positive (**+1.21 pp** in difference-in-differences, −3.39 to +5.81 pp; **+1.02 pp** in full *Double ML*, −2.44 to +4.47 pp). Detectable postsecondary costs concentrate when closure interrupts **secondary school (Grades 9 to 11, 1.º a 3.º Medio)**, right before graduation; in upper elementary I detect no loss in 2016–2019, although for 2009–2015 closures second-year persistence dips by −1.50 pp (*p* = 0.0499).
3. **The near-zero overall average conceals a net contraction in the vocational-technical pathway (IP/CFT) and a steep penalty in the quintile with largest predicted harm (G1):** university entry shows no average decline (−0.34 pp in 2016–2019 and −0.24 pp during the pandemic), nor does entry into highly selective university programs via PAES/PDT (+1.75 pp after adjusting for prior history). Where trajectories do shift is enrollment in **professional institutes and technical training centers (IP/CFT)**, which contracts by **−2.09 percentage points** even in the most demanding *Double ML* specification controlling for $$G-5$$ school history (standard error 0.72 pp, *p* = 0.0037, interval −3.50 to −0.68 pp), with statistically significant heterogeneity persisting ($$\hat{\beta}_2$$ = +0.425, *p* = 0.0177). Meanwhile, when evaluating impacts across students' individual academic backpacks, the **most vulnerable quintile (G1)**—composed almost entirely of secondary students (99.5%), mostly in the vocational-technical track (77.2%) and with lower prior attendance, concentrated in historically depressed high schools—experiences a drop of **−12.26 pp in overall higher-education entry**, **−8.02 pp in IP/CFT entry**, and **−11.48 pp in second-year persistence**, in the student-controls specification; adding $$G-5$$ school history shrinks G1's overall-entry drop to −0.26 pp.

## Spoonful 1: settling the debts of part III and unlocking the higher-education black box

### Two questions left open in the school trajectory

Before jumping to universities or technical institutes, I owed readers of [part III]({{ '/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/' | relative_url }}) two methodological stress tests. There I showed that, following a school closure in 2016–2019, **grade progression failure or school lag** (no enrollment or enrollment below the expected grade one year later) rose by **+5.29 percentage points** (+2.08 to +8.51 pp, with Manski 2003[^manski2003] partial-identification bounds of −1.15 to +10.98 pp). However, I noted that two crucial checks recommended by recent staggered difference-in-differences econometrics (Callaway and Sant'Anna 2021[^callaway2021]; Roth et al. 2023[^roth2023]) remained pending: measuring how sensitive that finding was if closed and open schools had not followed perfectly **parallel trends**, and testing what happened if I anchored the exposed student cohort **two years before closure ($$G-2$$)** instead of one year before ($$G-1$$).
{: .text-justify}

<aside class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**Methodological note in plain language — How can these two stress tests be understood without equations?**
{: .text-justify}

- **Two cars on a highway and the non-parallel trends test (simplified version of HonestDiD, Rambachan and Roth 2023[^rambachan2023]):** Imagine two cars driving at nearly identical speeds before a highway detour ($$G-4$$ to $$G-2$$), though never with robotic precision: there is always a slight wobble in the distance between them. Standard difference-in-differences assumes that, without the detour, the post-event wobble would have been exactly zero. A simplified version of the idea in Rambachan and Roth (2023) asks a much more transparent question: given the largest pre-closure wobble observed between cohorts, how many times larger would a post-closure trend deviation have to be—a multiplier called **$$M^*$$**—to wipe out the statistical significance of the estimated effect? If $$M^*$$ is 1, a simple repetition of historical wobble suffices to erase the finding; if $$M^*$$ exceeds 2, the result survives even if post-closure drift is more than twice as large as any pre-closure fluctuation.
- **The filter of who was already seated before the announcement (anchoring at $$G-2$$ versus $$G-1$$):** Measuring students enrolled in the school's final year ($$G-1$$) mixes two very different groups: **stable students ($$G-2$$ stayers)** who had been at the school for years, and **late entrants** who enrolled for the first time in $$G-1$$, often because they were already bouncing between schools. Separating the two reveals how much of the post-closure stumble hits the school's core student body versus how much reflects last-minute student churn.
</aside>

Table 1 resolves both open questions from part III. First, the rise in school progression failure is not an artifact of poorly aligned pre-trends: under the criterion of Rambachan and Roth (2023[^rambachan2023]), the effect remains statistically significant at the 95% level even if post-closure trend divergence were up to **2.54 times larger** than the upper bound of pre-closure wobble (2.54 times the prior 95% bound or, equivalently, 5.21 times the prior point drift). Second, restricting the sample to **stable $$G-2$$ stayers** lowers the effect from +5.29 to **+3.87 pp** (+1.04 to +6.69 pp), that is, 1.42 points less (27%); late entrants suffer a larger shock (+9.58 pp, while the remaining 2.87% of the 6,581 exposed corresponds to students without a $$G-2$$ record in the same school because they were enrolled in the school's entry grade). In short: the immediate school disruption after closure is real, though more moderate once prior mobility is netted out. That makes the next question all the more pressing: **does that short-run school stumble leave a permanent scar when it is time to enter higher education, or does it fade along the way?**
{: .text-justify}

<aside class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**Methodological note — How the econometric upgrades across parts I, II, and III connect to this series finale:** Updating all four installments under a unified empirical standard ties the entire research arc together: in **part I** (1992–2012), the Mundlak–Chamberlain decomposition separates the permanent smaller scale of exiting schools (−5.90 and −6.23 pupils per teacher) from their internal pre-closure contraction (−0.216 and −0.148 per year), while the out-of-sample discrete-time hazard reaches an AUC of 0.8517 (capturing 43.7% of closures in the top risk decile). In **part II** (2013–2025), Empirical Bayes shrinkage across 97.9% of closures with SIMCE (95 recovered closures; +0.0061 SD) and 95.7% with admissions (44 recovered closures; −0.0420 SD) rules out selection-driven gains, and McFadden's conditional logit across 9,093 individual transfers shows that families reallocate by physical proximity (median 1.83 km, $$\hat{\beta}_{\text{dist}}$$ = −0.4029 per km) and municipal boundaries (OR = 3.397) rather than destination SIMCE scores (OR = 0.794, not significant; mean realized change of −0.035 SD). Finally, in **part III** (2005–2025), the approximate trimming band ([+3.79; +6.80 pp]), Manski bounds in closures with at least 90% DS No. 315 record compliance ([+0.09; +7.84 pp]), and the −3.35 pp cushion when the receiving school outperforms the closed school in prior promotion explain why transition costs depend on the destination school's pedagogical capacity.
</aside>

### How I rebuilt the bridge to SIES and PAES without breaking privacy or license rules

To follow every student from their school desk into higher education, I assembled and audited three families of official administrative records totaling **57 microdata packages** linked through MINEDUC's masked individual identifier (MRUN, an encrypted national ID that enables longitudinal research without ever exposing a person's RUT or name):
{: .text-justify}

- **SIES Higher Education Enrollment (2007–2026, 20 complete years):** from 971,644 enrollment records in 2007 to 1,448,106 in 2026, with valid MRUN coverage exceeding 99.7% across the analytical window, distinguishing each year whether the student enrolled in a **University**, a **Professional Institute (IP)**, or a **Technical Training Center (CFT)**.
- **SIES Higher Education Graduates (2007–2025, 19 complete years):** from 107,018 annual graduation records in 2007 to 307,988 in 2025, which makes it possible to verify actual degree completion for cohorts with a sufficient observation horizon.
- **PAES/PDT University Admission Tests (2021–2026, 6 annual cycles):** legacy DEMRE files for 2014–2020 carry a contractual clause that forbids individual-level cross-linkage without explicit written authorization from DEMRE; in strict compliance with that restriction, **I did not use any DEMRE 2014–2020 file**. Instead, I used exclusively the registration, score, application, and centralized enrollment microdata for the **2021 to 2026** admission cycles published directly by MINEDUC's Open Data portal under an **open CC-BY license**, which explicitly authorizes verifiable scientific reuse.

<aside class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**Methodological note — Why I audited the code on real data and why I compare against clean open schools:** Connecting these real administrative rosters to the computer module prepared at the close of part III uncovered **three technical lessons** worth sharing because they illustrate why data science should never trust code tested only on simulations:
{: .text-justify}

1. **A database table-binding bug:** an internal subquery omitted the origin-school key when filtering receiving schools, causing execution to halt the moment real data arrived.
2. **A computational bottleneck:** the legacy query grouped roughly 35 million student-year records in candidate grades before filtering relevant schools and parsed twenty compressed CSV files line by line. Converting the SIES and PAES sources into a fast columnar storage format (Apache Parquet) cut total execution time from hours to **under 25 seconds**.
3. **The trap of searching for an identical twin inside a room of twelve people (why matching only against future closing high schools fails):** in an idealized staggered design, researchers sometimes try to compare high schools that closed in 2016–2019 solely against other high schools that will close many years later (2021–2025), hoping both groups are more alike. In Chile's real administrative data, however, arithmetic is unforgiving: between 2016 and 2019 only **19 regular secondary schools** closed, and barely **12 secondary schools close in 2021–2025 in the entire country**. If one requires exact matching across **8 simultaneous dimensions** (year, grade, sex, age, region, governance, rurality, and vocational track) and requires at least two schools and five students in each of the four arms, **exactly 0 comparable cells remain** (0% retention; without matching, that comparison yields +4.37 pp, of opposite sign). It is like trying to find an identical twin of the same age, municipality, and specialization inside a room of twelve people. By contrast, matching within those exact same cells against **clean schools that remained open**—excluding receiving schools and locally affected markets, just as in my primary design in part III—recovers a solid common support of **188 exact cells and 2,223 treated secondary students** (273 cells and 2,953 treated in the broad check) and **123 cells with 935 treated students in upper elementary**.
</aside>

## Spoonful 2: the 13-point illusion and the causal decomposition ladder

### Separating the backpack the student already carried from the blow of the closure

I now turn to the core empirical question. For every student enrolled between **seventh and eleventh grade** in the year before their school closed ($$G-1$$), I computed their **expected higher-education entry year ($$t_0$$)** by adding the years remaining to graduate from twelfth grade on time (for instance, a student in 11th Grade, 3.º Medio, in 2017 whose school closed at the end of that year was expected to complete 12th Grade in 2018 and enter higher education in $$t_0$$ = 2019, or at latest in $$t_0 + 1$$ = 2020). Measuring the **two-year entry window ($$t_0$$ or $$t_0 + 1$$)** is essential in Chile, where many young people work for a year, retake the admission exam, or defer enrollment by twelve months without abandoning higher education.
{: .text-justify}

If I look only at the post-graduation snapshot for the primary **2016–2019** closure window, secondary students (1.º a 3.º Medio) whose high school closed enter higher education at a markedly lower rate than peers from open schools in the same region, governance, rurality, grade, and track: **50.61% versus 65.24%**, a raw cell gap of **−14.64 percentage points** (or **−13.24 pp** when estimated via *Double Machine Learning* adjusting only for demographics and school structure).
{: .text-justify}

Before blaming the closure for those thirteen or fourteen points, however, it helps to ask two basic questions:
{: .text-justify}

1. **What did the grades and attendance of those students look like *before* their high school closed?** This is their **individual academic backpack ($$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$)**: their grade-point average (which feeds into university admission via NEM), their classroom attendance rate, prior grade repetitions, and whether they were stable students from $$G-2$$ or had just arrived in $$G-1$$.
2. **What happened in that very same high school among cohorts that studied there *four years before the exposed cohort ($$G-5$$)*, while the school was still operating normally?** This is the **school's historical baseline**. If graduates from that high school were already entering higher education 12 points less four years before the exposed cohort, blaming those 12 points on the subsequent closure would be like blaming the second-half referee for goals conceded in the first half.

<aside class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**Methodological note in plain language — Belt and suspenders: if one fails, the other holds (Repeated-cohort DiD $$G-1$$ vs. $$G-5$$ and Double Machine Learning):** To peel these layers rigorously without relying on rigid linear functional forms, I combined two independent identification strategies so that if the assumptions of one are strained, the other holds up the conclusion:
{: .text-justify}

- **The belt — Repeated-cohort difference-in-differences ($$G-1$$ vs. $$G-5$$ within exact cells):** Because a student does not enter higher education for the first time twice (before and after their high school closes), I compare two generations from the **same high school**: the cohort exposed to closure in $$G-1$$ versus the $$G-5$$ cohort that studied there four years before the exposed cohort and, in secondary school, graduated before closure occurred. I then subtract the change observed between those same two cohorts in control schools matched exactly on region, rurality, governance, grade, sex, and age. Any permanent historical disadvantage of the closing school cancels out by subtraction. This comparison assumes that, without closure, the difference between the $$G-5$$ and $$G-1$$ cohorts would have been the same in closing and control high schools. Because closing high schools were already shrinking (part I), that assumption may fail; with only one pre-period I cannot test it here, and the test in table 1 pertains to the school outcomes in part III, not higher education.
- **The suspenders — Double/Debiased Machine Learning (with school-clustered cross-fitting):** What if, in addition to comparing school cohorts, I account flexibly for non-linear differences in GPA, attendance, age-for-grade, and prior mobility for every individual student? Here I deploy *Double/Debiased Machine Learning* (Chernozhukov et al. 2018[^chernozhukov2018]; Chernozhukov et al. 2024[^chernozhukov2024]). In plain terms, it works in three steps: (1) a flexible boosted-tree algorithm (HGB) and an interpretable glassbox additive model (EBM) predict expected higher-education entry given the student's and school's pre-closure backpack; (2) a regularized propensity model predicts the probability of attending a closing school given that same backpack; and (3) I compare only the **orthogonalized residuals** (what could not be explained by pre-existing characteristics). To prevent algorithms from memorizing individual schools, every model is trained using **5-fold school-clustered cross-fitting**: learners train on 80% of schools and predict out-of-sample on the held-out 20%.
</aside>

Table 2 deserves a pause. Had I stopped at rung 1—as happens whenever closed and open schools are compared without looking at their past—I would have falsely claimed that closing a high school slashes higher-education entry by 13.24 percentage points. Moving to rung 2 shows that 57.5% of that distance (−7.61 of −13.24 pp) arose because students at closing high schools already had lower grades and attendance before closure. Looking at rung 3a confirms that four years before the exposed cohort ($$G-5$$) those same high schools already posted a nearly identical gap (−12.00 of the −14.64 pp raw cell gap, or 82.0%; both percentages come from different samples and do not add up to more than 100% because individual academic fragility and school-level historical disadvantage strongly overlap). And when both pieces are combined in rung 3b, histogram-boosted trees (−0.29 pp), the interpretable additive model (−0.91 pp), and the Hájek doubly robust estimator (+0.19 pp) all converge on the same conclusion: **on overall average entry into any higher-education institution, I detect no additional drop distinguishable from zero beyond the historical disadvantage already borne by those schools and students (the interval rules out drops larger than 2.64 pp); yet entry into professional institutes and technical training centers (IP/CFT) contracts by −2.09 percentage points (*p* = 0.0037)**. That −2.09 pp does not come from a different model: it is the average coefficient ($$\beta_1$$) of the R-Learner's BLP regression, estimated on the same orthogonalized residuals as rung 3b but with IP/CFT entry as the outcome, so it nets out exactly the same student backpack and $$G-5$$ school history.
{: .text-justify}

Does this conclusion change if I separate students who were already enrolled two years prior ($$G-2$$ stayers) from late entrants arriving in the final year ($$G-1$$)? I tested that split with full *Double ML* as well: among stable $$G-2$$ stayers in secondary education (29,683 students, 1,974 treated), the initial −13.23 pp gap becomes **−1.50 pp** (SE 1.59 pp) once individual and school histories are netted out; and among late entrants arriving in $$G-1$$ (8,842 students, 1,264 treated), the initial −7.70 pp gap becomes **+1.50 pp** (SE 2.02 pp). The average gap on overall access is absorbed in both subgroups once prior trajectories are controlled for, which motivates the third spoonful on how multiple simultaneous vulnerabilities interact.
{: .text-justify}

2. **From the 2016 closures onward, the point estimate for IP/CFT turns negative, but no regime-specific difference is distinguishable from zero:** the DiD effect on IP/CFT entry moves from +0.22 pp in 2009–2015 closures to −2.59 pp in 2016–2019 (SE 2.29; *p* = 0.28; 12 clusters) and to −4.33 pp in 2020–2022 closures (SE 2.88; *p* = 0.18; 7 clusters), while university entry stays near zero (−0.34 and −0.24 pp) and second-year persistence falls −4.72 pp in the pandemic (SE 2.60; *p* = 0.12). Had I used the naive directory closure definition (D1) without purging student flows (D2), I would have mixed block campus relocations boasting 71.60% and 81.10% college entry with genuine school closures at 50.61%, severely biasing any causal inference.

## Spoonful 3: when the average hides the losers — Causal ML and PAES/PDT admission

### The oven-and-freezer paradox: how the Orthogonal R-Learner (BLP, GATE, and CLAN) works

An old statisticians' joke notes that if a person has their head in a hot oven and their feet in a freezer, on average their body temperature is comfortable, yet in reality they are in grave danger. The exact same principle applies to causal inference: finding that the overall average effect on higher-education entry is close to zero once school history is netted out does not imply that closure is harmless for every student.
{: .text-justify}

<aside class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**Methodological note in plain language — How the Orthogonal R-Learner works in three stages (CATE, BLP, GATE, and CLAN):** How can one search for effect differences across student profiles without falling into the trap of hand-picking a hundred subgroups until one turns up significant by chance (*p-hacking*)? Here I deploy the **Orthogonal R-Learner** of Nie and Wager (2021[^niewager2021]) and Wager (2024[^wager2024]), building on generalized random forests (Athey, Tibshirani, and Wager 2019[^athey2019]) and paired with the generic machine-learning inference framework **BLP / GATE / CLAN** of Chernozhukov, Demirer, Duflo, and Fernández-Val (2025[^cddf2018]) and Chernozhukov et al. (2024[^chernozhukov2024], chs. 4 and 14)—originally developed for randomized experiments with known propensity scores and applied here with cross-fitted estimated propensities. In plain language, the procedure operates like a three-stage medical diagnostic that strictly separates learning from validation:
{: .text-justify}

1. **Estimating an individual vulnerability signal (CATE, Conditional Average Treatment Effect) without memorizing schools:** Using the orthogonalized residuals that already purged pre-closure history in the previous step, a second regularized model (trained via 5-fold cross-fitting clustered by origin school) learns how the closure impact varies with individual student attributes in $$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$ (school level, vocational-technical track, GPA, attendance, prior grade repetitions, and $$G-2$$ stayer status). Restricting this second stage strictly to student-level moderators—and never school size or school IDs—is a crucial methodological safeguard: it prevents the algorithm from memorizing the small number of closed high schools and guarantees that each student's predicted vulnerability comes strictly from patterns learned in other schools.
2. **The smoke-detector test (BLP, *Best Linear Predictor*):** A reliable smoke detector should ring when there is real fire, not when someone makes toast. Before trusting the groups formed by the machine, I run a weighted regression with school-clustered standard errors that estimates two numbers: the average effect ($$\beta_1$$) and the heterogeneity slope (**$$\beta_2$$**). If **$$\beta_2$$ is zero**, the alarm stays silent: differences across students were mere statistical noise. If **$$\beta_2$$ is positive and statistically significant (*p* < 0.05)**, the alarm confirms that the impact of school closure genuinely varies systematically across student profiles.
3. **The five impact tiers (GATE, G1 to G5) and the portrait of who sits at each extreme (CLAN):** Once the signal is validated, I sort students into five equal-sized quintiles according to their predicted vulnerability—from the **most harmed 20% (G1)** to the **most resilient 20% (G5)**—estimate the actual causal effect inside each quintile (**GATE**, *Group Average Treatment Effects*), and finally compare the average characteristics of students in G1 against those in G5 (**CLAN**, *Classification Analysis*).
</aside>

Look closely at the two levels of evidence revealed by table 4. At the first level, when I control for the student's entire individual academic backpack ($$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$) while allowing the algorithm to learn how grade at closure, vocational-technical track, attendance, and prior stability interact, the BLP test triggers an alarm across three key outcomes: there is genuine heterogeneity in overall higher-education entry ($$\hat{\beta}_2$$ = +0.634, *p* = 0.0243), in second-year persistence ($$\hat{\beta}_2$$ = +0.580, *p* = 0.0247), and, with even stronger statistical precision, in entry into **professional institutes and technical training centers (IP/CFT, $$\hat{\beta}_2$$ = +0.800, *p* = 0.0007)**. At the second level, when I additionally condition on the origin school's historical $$G-5$$ rate, heterogeneity in overall entry is absorbed (G1 moves to −0.26 pp and the $$\hat{\beta}_2$$ slope is no longer significant)—indicating that much of the −12.26 pp drop occurred because students with the most fragile individual backpacks were concentrated inside historically depressed high schools—**yet in the vocational-technical IP/CFT channel both the average contraction (−2.09 pp, *p* = 0.0037) and the heterogeneity slope remain alive and statistically significant ($$\hat{\beta}_2$$ = +0.425, *p* = 0.0177, with drops of −2.57 pp in G1, −2.94 pp in G2, and −3.92 pp in G3)**, although the ordering across quintiles is not monotonic (G1 is not distinguishable from zero and the largest drop is in G3).
{: .text-justify}

Who makes up that 20% of students with the most vulnerable individual profile (G1)? The CLAN analysis paints their exact portrait without guesswork: **99.49%** of students in G1 were in **secondary school** when closure occurred (whereas in G5 only 13.75% were in secondary school and students who experienced closure in seventh or eighth grade predominate), **77.17%** belonged to the **vocational-technical track** (versus 0.28% in G5), their pre-closure GPA in $$G-1$$ was lower (5.33 versus 5.63), their pre-closure attendance in $$G-1$$ was already more fragile (**87.13%** versus **93.07%** in G5), they came from high schools whose prior $$G-5$$ cohort entered higher education less often (57.17% versus 66.63%), and immediately after closure they experienced greater school lag or dropout (**8.98%** versus **6.21%**). At the same time, and contrary to what one might assume, G1 has fewer prior grade repetitions (1.71% versus 15.09%) and a higher share of stable $$G-2$$ stayers (77.12% versus 64.87%).
{: .text-justify}

### What happens with the PAES/PDT admission test and entry into the most selective programs?

One piece of the puzzle remains: does school closure affect the probability of sitting for the national admission exam (PDT in 2021–2022 and PAES since 2023), submitting a centralized application, enrolling through that system, or entering the **top 20% most selective degree programs in the country**?
{: .text-justify}

<aside class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**Methodological note in plain language — Penalty shootouts and Empirical Bayes selectivity shrinkage:** To measure program selectivity without being misled by small-enrollment noise (where three high scores in a single year can make a ten-seat program look ultra-selective), I built an **Empirical Bayes shrinkage selectivity index** over the official PAES/PDT enrollment microdata for 2021–2026. In plain terms: if a soccer player takes only 2 penalty kicks and scores both (100%), nobody would claim they have better proven accuracy than a specialist who took 100 penalties and scored 85 (85%). *Empirical Bayes* performs that exact prudent adjustment: it blends the average admission score of students enrolled in each program (program code by year) with the system-wide mean according to program size, and then identifies students who enroll in the **top quintile (top 20%) of most demanding programs nationwide**.

The contrast between table 5 and the preceding tables illuminates a crucial institutional mechanism in Chilean education. Students who aspire to selective university programs and reach the end of twelfth grade with strong grades and attendance sit for the PAES and apply without a drop attributable to closure once prior history is controlled for (+1.75 pp, not significant, in top-20% selectivity and +4.30 pp in application). That positive sign on application (+4.30 pp) has no established explanation: it may reflect over-adjustment when conditioning on $$G-5$$ history in high schools with a very low historical baseline, and I treat it as an anomaly warranting caution rather than evidence of resilience.
{: .text-justify}

Why, then, does enrollment in **professional institutes and technical training centers (IP/CFT)** contract? First, it is necessary to consider a rival explanation: in the same specification, university entry rises by +1.98 pp (−0.22 to +4.19) while overall higher-education entry is unchanged, so part of the IP/CFT contraction could reflect substitution toward universities rather than a net loss of access. The aggregate arithmetic settles nothing either way: the point estimates of the two channels nearly cancel out, which is consistent with broad substitution, yet the university interval includes zero, which is consistent with a net loss. The discriminating test lies in individual trajectories: if displaced students who forgo an IP or CFT appear enrolled in universities, substitution dominates; if they do not enroll anywhere, net loss dominates. That test is left for a future installment; in the meantime, I read the −2.09 pp as a contraction of the IP/CFT channel, not as a proven loss of access. My explanatory hypothesis for the non-substituted portion—which is consistent with the contrast observed between the two channels, even though administrative records do not directly measure internal school counseling—is that transitioning into an IP or CFT does not run through a single national portal like the PAES: it relies on direct enrollment right after twelfth grade, on school-specific vocational-technical articulation networks (internship agreements, guidance from trade teachers, and local continuity), and on not losing administrative momentum when forced to switch high schools. When a high school closes in the middle of Grades 9 to 11, it is precisely that web of vocational-technical articulation that gets severed.
{: .text-justify}

## Closing: what changes for public policy when selection is separated from causal effect

I thus reach the close of a four-part journey that began by revisiting the historical evidence from the 1990s and 2000s ([part I]({{ '/datos/educacion/politica-publica/replica-tesis-establecimientos-educacionales/' | relative_url }})), mapped which schools genuinely closed and how far away their alternatives stood ([part II]({{ '/datos/educacion/politica-publica/extender-tesis-no-es-agregar-anos/' | relative_url }})), reconstructed short-run school trajectories under the student-flow closure rule ([part III]({{ '/datos/educacion/politica-publica/costos-cierre-escolar-trayectorias/' | relative_url }})), and culminates today by following those cohorts all the way into higher education and degree completion between 2007 and 2026.
{: .text-justify}

Taken together, the three spoonfuls of this fourth installment deliver a dual message that avoids both naive alarmism and bureaucratic complacency:
{: .text-justify}

1. **Do not confuse the pre-existing fragility of closing high schools with the effect of closure, but do not use the near-zero average as an excuse either:** Over **80%** of the apparent −13.24 to −14.64 percentage-point gap in higher-education entry among displaced secondary students was already present four years before the exposed cohort ($$G-5$$: −12.00 pp in 2016–2019 and −15.35 pp in 2009–2015) and in the student's prior academic backpack ($$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$). Purging that selection via repeated-cohort difference-in-differences and *Double Machine Learning* prevents blaming school closure for deep-seated educational segregation that long preceded the event.
2. **Real postsecondary harm has a precise address—secondary education, the vocational-technical channel (IP/CFT, −2.09 pp), and the student quintile with the largest predicted harm (G1):** While in upper elementary I detect no loss of access in 2016–2019 (+1.21 pp in DiD, +1.02 pp in DML, although in 2009–2015 second-year persistence dips by −1.50 pp with *p* = 0.0499) and selective university entry via PAES/PDT does not contract holding prior history constant, students caught by a closure during secondary school see their entry into **professional institutes and technical training centers fall (−2.09 pp in full DML controlling for $$G-5$$ history, −2.59 pp in 2016–2019 DiD, and −4.33 pp during the pandemic)**, and the 20% for whom the model predicts the largest harm (G1: 99.5% in secondary school, 77.2% in the vocational-technical track, and prior attendance of 87.1% versus 93.1% in G5) register **−12.26 points lower entry** and **−11.48 points lower second-year persistence** in the student-controls specification; adding $$G-5$$ school history shrinks G1's overall-entry drop to −0.26 pp.

Translated into public-policy design, this finding shows that treating every school closure with a uniform administrative checklist is inefficient. When an elementary school closes, the priority is securing nearby seats, transport, and immediate pedagogical welcome to cushion the immediate school progression lag documented in part III (+3.87 to +5.29 points across all 2016–2019 closures). When closure hits a **secondary school—and especially a vocational-technical high school**—however, relocation cannot end with assigning a desk in March: it requires a **named postsecondary transition protocol toward vocational-technical higher education (IP/CFT)** for Grades 9 to 11 students with early-warning indicators of low prior attendance, especially in the vocational-technical track, guaranteeing specialty credit recognition, timely completion of the Single Socioeconomic Accreditation Form (FUAS) for tuition-free benefits and technical scholarships, and active follow-up across their first two postsecondary years. These precautionary safeguards are not estimated effects of those decisions; they are the targeted response once the evidence shows on which rung and for whom the bridge breaks. And if the individual-trajectory test showed that the IP/CFT drop is mostly substitution toward universities, the protocol would shift its focus—from restoring access to accompanying a choice—without losing its rationale.
{: .text-justify}

### 1. Cohort definition, postsecondary horizon, and the clean closure rule (RCID v1)

The analytical universe starts from school closure episodes identified between **2005 and 2024** across MINEDUC's longitudinal panel (2004–2025, 79,709,054 enrollment records and 73,873,835 performance records). Following the hierarchy established in part III ($$D_0 \supset D_1 \supset D_2$$), I distinguish:
{: .text-justify}

- **$$D_0$$ (1,981 raw exits 2005–2025):** any school with positive enrollment in $$t-1$$ that records zero enrollment from $$t$$ onward and does not reopen.
- **$$D_1$$ (1,807 strict directory closures):** purges annexes, secondary campuses, and direct administrative code (RBD) continuities.
- **$$D_2$$ or RCID v1 (1,678 closures in the conservative version and 1,648 clean closures with observed $$t+1$$ window):** further purges **concentrated student-flow absorptions** (schools with at least 10 students where a single receiving school absorbs 70% or more of the displaced cohort with concentration index HHI of at least 0.50 and the same modal destination in $$t$$ and $$t+1$$). In terms of the Rubin Causal Model (Imbens and Rubin 2015[^imbensrubin2015]), this filtering mitigates both sources of violation of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA): (i) *hidden versions of treatment* (separating genuine closures from block campus relocations $$D_1 \setminus D_2$$) and (ii) *interference* in the control group (excluding receiving schools and local municipality-level markets directly exposed to closure).

To evaluate trajectories into higher education across the full theory-of-change causal chain (Gertler et al. 2017[^gertler2017]), I focus on students enrolled in the year prior to closure ($$G-1$$) in **upper elementary (7.º or 8.º Básico, teaching code 110)** or **regular secondary education (1.º a 3.º Medio, academic code 310, vocational-technical codes 410–810, and artistic code 910)**, and exclude cells whose expected entry year $$t_0$$ falls outside 2007–2025 (so that $$t_0 + 1$$ is contained within 2007–2026). Let $$g_i$$ (from 7 to 11) denote the ordinal grade attended by student $$i$$ in year $$G - 1$$. Their **expected on-time twelfth-grade graduation year** is $$(G - 1) + (12 - g_i)$$, and their **expected on-time higher-education entry year ($$t_0$$)** is defined by:
{: .text-justify}

$$
t_0(i) = G + (12 - g_i)
$$

For example, for a student in 3.º Medio ($$g_i$$ = 11) in $$G - 1$$, their expected entry year is $$t_0 = G + 1$$; for a student in 7.º Básico ($$g_i$$ = 7) in $$G - 1$$, it is $$t_0 = G + 5$$. On this individual clock aligned by origin grade, I define six postsecondary outcomes in the SIES individual rosters (2007–2026): (1) **on-time entry in $$t_0$$**; (2) **two-year window entry ($$t_0$$ or $$t_0 + 1$$)**, the primary outcome because it captures students who defer enrollment by one year; (3) **university entry in $$t_0$$ or $$t_0 + 1$$**; (4) **entry into a Professional Institute (IP) or Technical Training Center (CFT) in $$t_0$$ or $$t_0 + 1$$**; (5) **two-year consecutive persistence** (being enrolled in higher education both in the initial entry year and in the immediately following year); and (6) **cumulative graduation through 2025** in the official SIES Graduation rosters (2007–2025), right-censored when $$t_0 \ge 2019$$.
{: .text-justify}

### 2. Repeated-cohort econometric specification ($$G-1$$ vs. $$G-5$$) and cluster-robust inference

In the repeated-cohort difference-in-differences design within exact cells, each cell $$c$$ is defined by the exact combination of: closure episode year $$G$$, origin level and grade $$g_i$$, academic versus vocational-technical track, school region, governance type, and rurality. Following the principle of never conditioning on post-treatment *bad controls* (Angrist and Pischke 2009[^angristpischke2009]; Huntington-Klein 2025[^huntingtonklein2025]), all controls are fixed strictly at $$G-1$$, $$G-2$$, and $$G-5$$, excluding intermediate post-closure variables ($$G$$ or $$G+1$$) that would block part of the causal channel. Within each cell $$c$$, I observe four arms: students from the closing school in the exposed cohort ($$G-1$$, post-treated arm) and in an earlier cohort from the **same school** four years before the exposed cohort ($$G-5$$, pre-treated arm: in secondary school it graduates in $$G-2$$ if enrolled in 1.º Medio, in $$G-3$$ if in 2.º Medio, and in $$G-4$$ if in 3.º Medio; in upper elementary, the $$G-5$$ cohort of 8.º Básico attends 4.º Medio in $$G-1$$ and that of 7.º Básico only reaches 3.º Medio in $$G-1$$), compared against the $$G-1$$ and $$G-5$$ cohorts of clean never-treated schools (excluding receiving schools and local municipality-level markets directly exposed to closure). The estimator weighted by the number of exposed students in $$G-1$$ ($$N_{1\text{post}(c)}$$) is:
{: .text-justify}

$$
\hat{\Delta}_{\text{DiD}} = \sum_{c} w_c \left[ \left(\bar{Y}_{\text{trat}(G-1)c} - \bar{Y}_{\text{trat}(G-5)c}\right) - \left(\bar{Y}_{\text{ctrl}(G-1)c} - \bar{Y}_{\text{ctrl}(G-5)c}\right) \right]
$$

with weights $$w_c = N_{1\text{post}(c)} / \sum_{k} N_{1\text{post}(k)}$$. Variance is estimated by clustering at the origin school/episode level. Because the 2016–2019 design rests on 12 treated clusters in secondary and 11 in upper elementary (7 and 4 in pandemic closures), I complement analytical inference with a six-point Webb *wild cluster bootstrap* (MacKinnon and Webb 2017[^mackinnonwebb2017]; Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller 2008[^cameron2008]) and a Student $$t$$ distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of treated clusters minus one (Angrist and Pischke 2009[^angristpischke2009]; that number is not the closure year $$G$$ of the school clock), reporting the more conservative interval. The pandemic upper-elementary row (4 clusters) is purely descriptive. One caveat specific to upper elementary: in schools offering both elementary and secondary grades, the $$G-5$$ cohort of 7.º Básico was attending 3.º Medio in the school's final year, so it is not a pure pre-treated arm; if closure also affected it, the upper-elementary DiD is biased toward zero, which is why I read its +1.21 pp as absence of detectable harm rather than as a benefit. In the retrospective evaluation of non-parallel trends inspired by Rambachan and Roth (2023[^rambachan2023]) on part III (table 1), alongside the ratios relative to the prior 95% bound (2.54 times) and the prior point drift (5.21 times), if the post-closure slope change is instead bounded by the largest pre-closure slope change (0.15 pp per year), the interval withstands up to 14.24 times that change; these thresholds are a simplified single-post-period version rather than the output of the formal procedure.
{: .text-justify}

### 3. Causal Machine Learning specification (DML PLR, Hájek DR-ATT, and Orthogonal R-Learner)

To flexibly purge both the student's prior academic backpack ($$X_{\text{acad}}$$: grade-point average in $$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$, attendance rate in $$G-1$$ and $$G-2$$, prior grade repetitions, and $$G-2$$ stayer indicator) and the school's historical baseline ($$X_{\text{school}}$$: overall, university, and vocational-technical entry rates of the $$G-5$$ cohort from the same school), I estimate two complementary **Double/Debiased Machine Learning** models (Chernozhukov et al. 2018[^chernozhukov2018]; Chernozhukov et al. 2024[^chernozhukov2024]; Ding 2024[^ding2024]) using **5-fold cross-fitting grouped by origin school**:
{: .text-justify}

1. **Robinson Partially Linear Model (DML PLR):** For each of the five folds $$k$$ (from 1 to 5), I train on the remaining four folds an outcome predictor $$\hat{g}_{-k}(X_i)$$ via histogram-boosted trees (*HistGradientBoostingRegressor*) and, in parallel, an interpretable generalized additive model (*ExplainableBoostingRegressor*, EBM *glassbox*), alongside a regularized propensity model $$\hat{m}_{-k}(X_i)$$ bounded in [0.01; 0.85] (Imbens and Rubin 2015[^imbensrubin2015]; Ding 2024[^ding2024]); clipping does not create overlap, it only limits extreme weights (with a minimum observed propensity of 0.0100 in the steps with student and school controls). Using out-of-fold residuals $$\tilde{u}_i = Y_i - \hat{g}_{-k(i)}(X_i)$$ and $$\tilde{v}_i = D_i - \hat{m}_{-k(i)}(X_i)$$, the Neyman-orthogonal estimator is:

   $$
   \hat{\theta}_{\text{PLR}} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{v}_i \tilde{u}_i\right) / \left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{v}_i^2\right)
   $$

   with school-clustered standard errors derived from the orthogonal influence function $$\psi_i$$. Under heterogeneous effects, $$\hat{\theta}_{\text{PLR}}$$ is an average of conditional effects weighted by conditional treatment variance given $$X$$, not the average effect on the treated; for that reason I also report the Hájek DR-ATT.
2. **Hájek-normalized Doubly Robust Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (DR-ATT):** Combines the counterfactual outcome predictor trained strictly on controls $$\hat{g}_{0(-k)}(X_i)$$ with renormalized inverse-probability weights (Ding 2024[^ding2024]):

   $$
   w_{c(i)} = \left[(1 - D_i) \hat{m}_{-k}(X_i) / \left(1 - \hat{m}_{-k}(X_i)\right)\right] / \left[\sum_{j=1}^{N} (1 - D_j) \hat{m}_{-k}(X_j) / \left(1 - \hat{m}_{-k}(X_j)\right)\right]
   $$

   This estimator remains consistent if either the outcome model *or* the propensity model is correctly specified.
3. **Heterogeneity via Orthogonal R-Learner and BLP / GATE / CLAN tests:** In the second stage, I estimate the conditional treatment effect function $$\hat{\tau}(Z_i)$$ by minimizing Robinson's weighted squared loss $$\sum_i \tilde{v}_i^2 \left[ (\tilde{u}_i / \tilde{v}_i) - \tau(Z_i) \right]^2$$ with school-clustered cross-fitting over the individual moderator vector $$Z_i$$ (secondary versus elementary level, ordinal grade, vocational-technical track, GPA, attendance, prior repetitions, and $$G-2$$ stayer status). Using out-of-fold predictions $$S_i = \hat{\tau}_{-k(i)}(Z_i)$$, the orthogonal Best Linear Predictor (BLP) calibration regression:

   $$
   \tilde{u}_i = \beta_1 \tilde{v}_i + \beta_2 \tilde{v}_i \left(S_i - \bar{S}\right) + \varepsilon_i
   $$

   delivers the formal heterogeneity test ($$H_0: \beta_2 = 0$$), while projecting onto indicators for the five quintiles of $$S_i$$ yields group average effects (GATE G1 to G5).

### 4. Formal omitted-variable sensitivity analysis of Cinelli and Hazlett (2020)

What if an unobserved family factor absent from MINEDUC registries—such as household income, parental education, or home support—explained part of the drop in IP/CFT entry or the differences across students? I apply the formal omitted-variable bias sensitivity framework of **Cinelli and Hazlett (2020[^cinellihazlett2020])**, which computes the **Robustness Value ($$\text{RV}$$)**—the percentage of residual variance in both treatment and outcome that an unobserved confounder would need to explain to drive the effect to zero—and bounds the maximum bias if an omitted confounder were as strong as the most powerful observed covariates (student GPA NEM, attendance, or $$G-5$$ school history). In plain terms: the unobserved family factor would have to explain more than twice as much of school closure as the school's $$G-5$$ history—the strongest observed covariate—just for the interval to touch zero, and considerably more to nullify the point estimate; possible, but not just any confounder.
